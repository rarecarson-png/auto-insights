ASSIGNMENT 01

Research question & proposal

Research plan

Research topic

The relationship between vehicle characteristics and manufacturer suggested retail price, or MSRP.

The real-world problem

Car buyers compare vehicles with different brands, engine specifications, body styles, and fuel economy ratings. These differences make it difficult to understand how listed prices relate to measurable features.

Why this matters

Understanding price patterns could help consumers compare vehicles and help marketers explain how products are positioned across different market segments.

This project examines the field labeled MSRP in the selected dataset. It does not estimate current dealership prices, used-car resale values, or the total cost of ownership. Price definitions and unusual values require further source validation.

PRIMARY RESEARCH QUESTION

How do vehicle specifications and brand relate to MSRP in the Car Features and MSRP dataset?

Research sub-questions

  1. How does MSRP vary across vehicle brands?
  2. What relationship exists between horsepower and MSRP?
  3. How does MSRP differ across body styles and vehicle sizes?
  4. How do city and highway fuel economy relate to MSRP?
  5. Does the relationship between horsepower and MSRP remain after accounting for brand and model year?

Preliminary hypotheses

H1: Vehicles with higher horsepower will tend to have higher MSRP.

H2: MSRP will differ across brands, even after accounting for horsepower and model year.

These are predictions to test, not established findings. Observed associations will not establish causation.

Project introduction

My project is Auto Insights. I selected Cooper Union’s Car Features and MSRP dataset from Kaggle to investigate how vehicle specifications and brand relate to recorded prices.

I chose this topic because comparing cars provides a practical way to connect data analysis with consumer decisions. The dataset combines prices, horsepower, fuel economy, brand, and vehicle categories, which support several types of visual comparisons.

The problem is price transparency: buyers face many specifications and price differences without an easy way to compare them. My project explains patterns in this historical dataset. It does not provide current purchase prices or personalized buying advice.

RESEARCH PLAN

Goals and experimental design

What I want to accomplish

  1. Describe how recorded MSRP differs across brands, vehicle sizes, and performance levels.
  2. Identify data-quality issues that affect those comparisons.
  3. Compare three predictive models to estimate recorded MSRP from available vehicle characteristics.
  4. Build visual explanations of model errors and feature relationships.
  5. Document the methods, results, limitations, and conclusions in a research paper.

Planned evaluation

The prediction target will be recorded MSRP. Candidate predictors include brand, model year, horsepower, cylinders, vehicle size and style, transmission, and driven wheels. Fuel-economy fields will require unit checks before inclusion.

I will reserve approximately 20% of the data for testing, keeping each make–model group in only one split so related trims do not appear in both training and testing. I will tune models using grouped cross-validation within the training set.

Preprocessing will be fitted on training data only. Missing-value handling and category encoding will be applied consistently. MSRP-derived columns, including log10_msrp and price-outlier flags, will not be used as input features.

How I will measure performance

I will report MAE (average absolute error in dollars), RMSE (which gives larger errors more weight), and R² on the same held-out test records. A training-set median-price predictor will provide a simple baseline. I will also inspect predicted-versus-observed plots and errors by vehicle size, year, and price range.

Sensitivity checks will compare results with and without the $2,000 price-floor records and extreme-price records. These checks will be reported separately, with the same evaluation rules and explicit sample sizes.

AI / MACHINE LEARNING

Three planned prediction models

Planned · not yet trained

1. Linear Regression

Estimates price using a weighted combination of vehicle features. This gives a straightforward starting point for evaluating whether an approximately linear relationship is useful.

Purpose: Provide an interpretable comparison model. Encoded categories and feature relationships require careful interpretation.

2. Decision Tree Regressor

Splits records into groups using feature thresholds and categories represented numerically. Each terminal group produces a predicted price.

Purpose: Explore nonlinear relationships and feature combinations. Tree depth and minimum leaf size will be controlled to reduce overfitting.

3. Random Forest Regressor

Combines predictions from many decision trees trained with randomness in their data and feature choices.

Purpose: Test whether combining trees improves held-out prediction accuracy over a single tree and linear regression.

These are three supervised regression models, plus a simple median-price baseline. No model scores are reported yet because training and evaluation have not been completed.

SOCIETAL PROBLEM AND CONTRIBUTION

Making vehicle comparisons easier to understand

The societal problem

Vehicle affordability and unclear price comparisons affect consumers. Buyers need understandable information about the relationship between price, performance, and vehicle categories. Historical data illustrates these patterns, but it does not establish whether a current listing is fairly priced.

What I have developed

A website that connects a dataset explorer, data-quality documentation, reproducible cleaning, executed SQL analysis, and ten exploratory charts. Every important exploratory chart includes an observation, interpretation, and research implication.

Planned contribution to Data Visualization

I will connect vehicle comparisons with model-error visualizations, showing where predictions work well and where they fail. I will evaluate raw versus logarithmic price views, compare error patterns across groups, and make missing values and uncertain comparisons visible.

This is an applied visualization and evaluation project, not a claim to invent a new machine-learning algorithm. I have learned that skewed prices, uneven model-year coverage, and missingness change how a chart should be interpreted.

NEXT STEPS

From exploration to a research paper

  1. Validate the remaining data issues. Review the $2,000 price floor, unusually expensive vehicles, and electric-efficiency units.
  2. Prepare reproducible model experiments. Create grouped training and test splits, fit preprocessing on training data, and establish the median baseline.
  3. Train and compare the three models. Report held-out MAE, RMSE, R², and group-level errors without selecting models based on test performance.
  4. Develop final visualizations and an interactive dashboard. Add clear filters, readable labels, accessible colors, and views linking price patterns to prediction errors.
  5. Write and revise the research paper. Include the societal problem, research questions, dataset, methods, model experiments, visualization contribution, results, limitations, conclusions, and references.

The final paper will distinguish completed evidence from planned work, explain what the experiments show, and discuss how sampling and data-quality limitations affect the conclusions.
